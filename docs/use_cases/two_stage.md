# Two-Stage Probe Cascade

**Contributor:** [@rishabhsinha17](https://github.com/rishabhsinha17)

---

## What it does

Lowers the false-positive rate of a detector by adding a second check that only runs on the steps the first one flags. The final verdict flags a step only when both stages fire. Stage 1 is a cheap, high-recall gate. Stage 2 can be another probe on the same capture, or any slower scorer such as a guard model.

Requiring both stages can lower the false-positive rate of stage 1 but can never raise its recall, so calibrate the two thresholds together on held-out data. The second stage only helps if it sees something stage 1 does not.

---

## Pieces

`mia.utils.two_stage` (numpy only):

| Function | Use |
|---|---|
| `calibrate_cascade(s1, s2, y, target_recall, max_stage2_rate=None, finite_sample=False, stage1_recall=None)` | Lowest false-positive rate at a target recall. `max_stage2_rate` caps the share of steps sent to an expensive stage 2. `stage1_recall` fixes the stage-1 gate and searches only the stage-2 threshold, which holds recall better on new data. |
| `calibrate_cascade_for_fpr(s1, s2, y, target_fpr)` | Highest recall within a false-positive budget. |
| `threshold_for_recall(pos_scores, recall, finite_sample=False)` | Single-stage threshold for a target recall. `finite_sample=True` uses the split-conformal count. |
| `threshold_for_fpr(neg_scores, fpr)` | Single-stage threshold for a false-positive budget, from negatives alone such as one deployment's benign traffic. |
| `TwoStageDetector(stage1, stage2, t1, t2)` | Runs any two scorers and calls `stage2` only on the stage-1 flags. |
| `save_cascade_config(path, stage1_probe, stage2_probe, point, **meta)` | Writes the `cascade.json` the analyzer reads. |
| `tool_call_window(tokenizer, token_ids, max_tokens=64)` | Generated-token window of the first tool call in an action, for a `decode_window` probe. |

The `two_stage_probe` analyzer (worker `capture_hs`) runs linear probes in the `tool_call_risk` artifact format on one capture. A stage is one probe or a product of probes (`{"product": [...]}`). Each probe reads the last prompt token (`"position": "prompt_last"`, the default), the mean of the states for the first generated tokens (`"decode_mean"`), or their mean over a per-request window of generated tokens (`"decode_window"`), as set in its probe.json or the spec:

```python
from mia.utils.two_stage import calibrate_cascade, save_cascade_config

point = calibrate_cascade(s1_cal, s2_cal, y_cal, target_recall=0.9)   # held-out calibration scores
save_cascade_config("cascade.json", "stage1/probe.npz", "stage2/probe.npz", point)

out = llm.analyze(analyzer_spec={"cascade_path": "cascade.json"}, run_id=run_id)
# out["verdicts"], out["reached_stage2"], out["stage1"]["probabilities"], out["stage2"]["probabilities"]
```

Both thresholds must come from `cascade.json` or the spec. The analyzer ignores each `probe.json` threshold, because that one was set for the probe on its own. The infer config must capture every probe layer. `"score_all": true` scores stage 2 on every step, which recalibration needs. A `decode_mean` probe needs each request's own capture, so generate with `extra_args={"hooks_on": "both"}` and `merge_probes=False`, then pass `probes=[o.probes for o in outputs]`. The default merged capture keeps only the prompt passes.

## Evaluation on the tool-call risk probe

These numbers come from the AgentDojo steps behind the [tool-call risk probe](tool_call_risk.md), all from Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct: 1,729 attacked steps, 358 of them unsafe, and 97 clean steps with no injection. Folds group steps by user task, so training never sees a test task. Thresholds come from out-of-fold scores inside the training split, and every number is the mean of 4 fold seeds. Intervals are paired 95% bootstrap intervals over user tasks for the change from the released probe.

| Detector, 90% recall target | Recall | FPR, attacked but safe | FPR, clean |
|---|---|---|---|
| Released probe alone (layer 28) | 0.899 | 0.394 | 0.387 |
| Stage 2 = injection-presence probe on layer 28 | 0.898 [−0.007, +0.006] | 0.387 [−0.021, +0.003] | 0.134 [−0.335, −0.173] |
| Stage 2 = a second risk probe on layer 21 | 0.885 [−0.035, +0.004] | 0.383 [−0.031, +0.008] | 0.330 [−0.098, −0.015] |

The presence probe is trained only to tell steps with an injection in context from their clean twins, with no outcome label. Requiring it to agree removes about two thirds of the false alarms on clean steps at an unchanged recall on the arrival step, the first step with the injection in its context, and it reads the layer stage 1 already captures. It cannot help on attacked-but-safe steps, which contain an injection by definition. On the steps after that, with the injection still in the history, the cascade's recall is much lower than the risk probe's alone (see [Injections that stay in the context](#injections-that-stay-in-the-context)). At a 95% recall target the clean false-positive rate falls from 0.513 to 0.214. With a false-positive budget of 0.2, it falls from 0.175 to 0.077 while recall stays at 0.79 and the attacked-but-safe rate rises from 0.201 to 0.209. The gain is uneven across suites. Clean false-positive rates fall from 0.94 to 0.11 on slack and from 0.19 to 0.01 on travel, but only from 0.52 to 0.41 on banking and from 0.14 to 0.10 on workspace. With only 16 to 40 clean steps per suite, the per-suite rates are rough. Length alone separates attacked from clean steps at 0.52 AUROC, so prompt length does not explain the gain, and in a check on a three-layer version of the presence probe, removing length from its score left the gain unchanged.

A second risk probe barely helps, because the two probes rank steps alike (Spearman 0.81). Even thresholds tuned on the evaluated labels only lower the attacked-but-safe rate from 0.385 to 0.373. The released probe flags about 45% of safe steps whose next action is a tool call, attacked or clean, against 7 to 20% of steps that end in text. So reducing the attacked-but-safe false positives needs a stage 2 that sees the action itself, such as hidden states at the first generated tokens (`hooks_on="both"`), scored before the tool runs. In a simulation that keeps the released probe's real scores, a stage 2 with an AUROC of 0.9 among the flagged steps roughly halves that rate, and one with 0.7 barely moves it.

At one global threshold the false-positive rate also varies by tool suite, from 0.18 on workspace to 0.79 on slack. Thresholds set per suite with `threshold_for_fpr` even this out but lower pooled recall from 0.90 to 0.84 at the same budget.

The presence cascade cuts clean-step false alarms on all three models in split A, and on held-out tool suites for Qwen and Granite. Its recall cost on arrival steps stays within about two points in splits A and B, except Hermes in split A at 0.865. With a held-out attack template (all test steps attacked), its recall falls from 0.953 to 0.900 on Granite [−0.094, −0.019] and from 0.904 to 0.827 on Hermes [−0.137, −0.022].

The decision cascade in this table reads the first 16 generated tokens. Hermes gains on every split. Qwen gains in splits A and C, but on a held-out suite its thresholds do not transfer and the rate rises. Recalibrating only the thresholds on half of the new suite's user tasks brings Qwen back to 0.409 against 0.503 [−0.161, −0.032]. Granite does not gain. The next section looks at why, and at a stage that reads the call itself.

vLLM-Hook's own Attention Tracker analyzer can serve as stage 2 with no training. On Granite, with the head configuration in model_configs/attention_tracker and the user's task as the instruction span, every second step was scored. A cascade from the risk probe to one minus the tracker's score lowers clean-step false alarms from 0.578 to 0.318 [−0.377, −0.162] and attacked-but-safe ones from 0.550 to 0.482 [−0.118, −0.021], at recall 0.848 against 0.885 [−0.085, +0.003]. On the same steps its clean-step rate is within the interval of the trained presence probe's [−0.165, +0.057], and its attacked-but-safe rate is lower [−0.099, −0.002]. The tracker needs an eager engine in its score mode, and its Q/K mode spilled too much to disk for 5,000-token agent prompts.

Granite 3.1 needed two accommodations. Its chat template drops `tool_calls` and replaces the tool-calling instruction whenever a system message is given. So prior calls are written in its own `<|tool_call|>` syntax and that instruction is prepended. Even so, Granite never emitted `<|tool_call|>`, and its actions were read from any JSON call in the text. Strict parsing finds only 13 unsafe steps, too few to evaluate.

### Clean steps at every depth of a task

The clean steps above sit at one point of each task, the same depth as the attacked steps with the injection. Cutting each task's own ground-truth trajectory before its first call and after every call gives 337 more clean steps per model, with no injection anywhere. Scored by detectors trained and calibrated on the original steps of the other user tasks, they draw far more false alarms than the original clean steps. The risk probe alone flags 0.713 of them on Qwen, 0.670 on Granite and 0.790 on Hermes, against 0.405, 0.345 and 0.371 on the original clean steps. The presence cascade flags 0.336, 0.539 and 0.433, against 0.165, 0.211 and 0.144. It still lowers the risk probe's rate on every model (intervals [−0.435, −0.319], [−0.185, −0.077] and [−0.413, −0.295]).

Most of the presence cascade's excess is at a task's first step, before any tool has returned anything. There it flags 0.778, 1.000 and 0.933 of the steps. In AgentDojo's setting an injection can only arrive in a tool output, so a deployment like it can skip steps that hold none, which the caller has to do because the analyzer scores every request. This is a recommendation, not something the evaluation tested, and it does not apply when untrusted text reaches the prompt another way, such as retrieved documents. On the steps with at least one tool output the presence cascade flags 0.157, 0.352 and 0.231, still above the original clean-step rates on Granite and Hermes, and the risk probe alone still flags 0.606, 0.536 and 0.721.

Adding these steps to training passes criteria fixed before the run on two of the three models. In this test the presence probe and the two thresholds, calibrated together, also learn the clean steps of the training user tasks at every depth, while the risk probe stays as it is. No test task's steps are in training, with folds by user task (split A) and by injection task (split D), and the comparison is against the released presence cascade. To pass, the 95% interval of the change in false alarms on the new clean steps with held-out user tasks had to lie below zero both at the first step and after it. For recall, the interval of the change had to stay above −0.05 in both splits. For false alarms elsewhere, it had to stay below +0.05 on attacked-but-safe steps in both splits and on the original clean steps with held-out user tasks. The cascade described here would change only if all three models passed. Each cell below gives the presence cascade's false-positive rate, released and then retrained, or the 95% interval of the retrained cascade's recall change on arrival steps.

| Model | New clean steps (A) | First step (A) | After the first step (A) | Original clean steps (A) | Recall change (A) | Recall change (D) | Passes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Qwen2.5-7B | 0.336 to 0.044 | 0.778 to 0.015 | 0.157 to 0.055 | 0.165 to 0.134 | [−0.017, +0.002] | [+0.003, +0.063] | yes |
| Granite-3.1-8B | 0.539 to 0.022 | 1.000 to 0.000 | 0.352 to 0.030 | 0.211 to 0.093 | [−0.018, +0.014] | [−0.028, +0.005] | yes |
| Hermes-3-Llama-3.1-8B | 0.433 to 0.107 | 0.933 to 0.036 | 0.231 to 0.135 | 0.144 to 0.211 | [−0.096, +0.069] | [+0.014, +0.123] | no |

On Hermes the rate on the original clean steps rises [+0.018, +0.119], and the recall interval with held-out user tasks is too wide to show that recall holds. Its attacked-but-safe rate also rises a little in both splits ([+0.003, +0.034] and [+0.008, +0.046]), within the criteria. So the released presence cascade stays as it is. On all three models this retraining also loses recall on attacked steps after arrival, as the next subsection shows. Retraining the risk probe on the clean steps as well passes the same criteria on Qwen only. On Granite it misses by 0.001, with a recall interval whose lower end is −0.051 for held-out injection tasks.

Every attacked step in this data has a tool output, so at the first step the retrained presence probe saw only clean examples. Its near-zero rate there may rest on the same cue as skipping, the absence of any tool output, and if so it could miss injections that reach the prompt before any tool runs. This was not tested. That cue cannot explain the steps after the first, which all hold a tool output. Those new clean steps do sit at other depths of their tasks than the attacked steps, though, and the next subsection finds that part of this cut in false alarms reflects that layout. With held-out injection tasks the retrained cascade's recall on arrival steps is higher than the released one's on Qwen and Hermes and does not change measurably on Granite. With held-out user tasks it loses one to two points of recall against the risk probe alone on Qwen ([−0.024, −0.002]) and Granite ([−0.029, −0.002]), while the released cascade's intervals include zero on both. None of these comparisons is part of the criteria.

### Injections that stay in the context

Every attacked step above is an arrival step, the first step with the injection in its context. In a real run the injected tool output stays in the history while the agent goes on. Continuing each attacked step's ground-truth calls and cutting a step after every further call gives 2,994 more attacked steps per model over 75 of the 97 user tasks, as if the agent had ignored the injection so far. They use AgentDojo's own injections with no new attack text. Each model generated an action for every step that fit its context limit (2,957 or 2,958), and the labels follow the same rules as before. Before these steps were captured, the tests and the decision rule were committed. The 90% recall target is set on arrival steps, as above, and each cell is recall on held-out user tasks, averaged over 4 fold seeds.

| Model (unsafe arrival / after-arrival steps) | Presence cascade, arrival steps | Presence cascade, after arrival | After arrival, injection only in an earlier output (unsafe) | Risk probe alone, after arrival | Retrained presence cascade, after arrival |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Qwen2.5-7B (353 / 539) | 0.894 | 0.562 | 0.511 (443) | 0.896 | 0.350 |
| Granite-3.1-8B (150 / 112) | 0.882 | 0.569 | 0.380 (73) | 0.757 | 0.310 |
| Hermes-3-Llama-3.1-8B (104 / 120) | 0.865 | 0.683 | 0.634 (80) | 0.915 | 0.375 |

After arrival the presence cascade's recall is below the risk probe alone's by 0.33, 0.19 and 0.23, with 95% intervals over user tasks of [−0.425, −0.246], [−0.332, −0.069] and [−0.303, −0.167]. On Granite the risk probe itself also falls, from 0.893 at arrival to 0.757. Against the cascade's own arrival-step recall the intervals of the drop are [−0.419, −0.246], [−0.481, −0.160] and [−0.267, −0.104]. The presence probe learned only from arrival steps, whose latest tool output carries the injection, and it misses most often on steps whose latest output carries none, which fits a cue on the latest output but does not test it. Ranked against clean steps of the same user tasks at the same depths, the released presence probe separates these steps only weakly (AUROC 0.673, 0.686 and 0.721). Its lower attacked-but-safe rate on these steps (Qwen 0.303 against 0.713 for the risk probe alone) comes from the same misses, so it is not a gain.

The retrained presence probe from the previous subsection loses more recall after arrival than the released one on every model, with intervals of the change of [−0.266, −0.153], [−0.384, −0.149] and [−0.410, −0.189], and [−0.297, −0.164], [−0.309, −0.086] and [−0.279, −0.077] with held-out injection tasks. Against same-depth clean steps its AUROC changes by [−0.006, +0.029], [−0.054, −0.005] and [−0.045, +0.001]. So part of its cut in false alarms comes from cues tied to the layout of its attacked training steps, all of them arrival steps, and under the pre-registered rule it is adopted on no model. Down-weighting the new clean steps, aimed at Hermes' rise in false alarms on the original clean steps, does not change this, and Hermes still fails the criteria with it. Training on attacked steps at every depth as well (the presence probe and the risk probe both see them) brings recall after arrival to 0.901, 0.801 and 0.890, but it fails the criteria on all three models. False alarms rise on clean steps after the first on Qwen (0.157 to 0.240) and Hermes (0.231 to 0.337), on the original clean steps on Granite (0.211 to 0.330) and Hermes (0.144 to 0.216), and on attacked-but-safe arrival steps on Granite (0.341 to 0.465) and Hermes (0.329 to 0.442).

Carrying the presence score forward within a task brings recall after arrival back to about the risk probe's own. Stage 2 takes the highest presence score among the task's steps so far. This check was post hoc for Qwen, and its script was committed while the Granite capture for these steps was still running and before the Hermes one started. It fixed a test of the recall gain before the Granite and Hermes results, and the gain holds on all three models (intervals [+0.249, +0.425], [+0.079, +0.341] and [+0.174, +0.309]), but it did not set a bar for the cost. Using the released probe, recall after arrival is 0.898, 0.766 and 0.921. On Granite and Hermes its rates come within 0.03 of the risk probe alone's on every subset, since the released cascade already flags 1.000 and 0.933 of first steps there (0.778 on Qwen). On all three models it gives up most or all of the clean-step gain, with rates on the original clean steps of 0.320, 0.351 and 0.374 against 0.165, 0.211 and 0.144 (risk probe alone 0.405, 0.345 and 0.371). Using the retrained probe, which rarely passes a first step, recall after arrival is 0.895, 0.743 and 0.908. On clean steps at every depth its false-alarm rate is then 0.182, 0.132 and 0.339 against the released cascade's 0.336, 0.539 and 0.433, and on the original clean steps 0.168, 0.142 and 0.278 against 0.165, 0.211 and 0.144. That roughly doubles Hermes' rate there and depends on the first-step cue that was not tested, so this combination needs its own pre-registered test, which the next subsection reports. It needs either state for each task at the caller, because the analyzer scores each request on its own, or a reading of several positions in one prompt, which the analyzer does not do. A presence stage that passes these steps cannot lower their attacked-but-safe rate, which stays at about the risk probe's (0.714, 0.487 and 0.779 against 0.713, 0.483 and 0.774).

### Carrying presence forward, tested on new attack templates

AgentDojo includes attack templates that none of the steps above use. Two of them, `tool_knowledge` and `system_message`, applied to the same user and injection tasks, give 1,258 more arrival steps per model and 2,284 after-arrival steps, of which 2,254 to 2,260 fit the context limit. No probe trains on them. The new steps come from a held-out template and a held-out user task, though their injection tasks were in training, as in split A above. Here no detector flags a step without a tool output, as recommended above. The carried score is the highest presence score among the task's steps so far that hold a tool output, so the first step no longer enters it. Before these steps were captured, the criteria and a decision rule were committed. On the new after-arrival steps the lower end of the 95% interval of the carried detector's recall change against the risk probe alone had to stay above −0.05, and on the new arrival steps the same against the released cascade. The upper end of the interval of its change in false alarms against the released cascade had to stay below +0.05 on the original clean steps, on the clean steps at every depth with a tool output, and on the new attacked-but-safe arrival steps. The cascade described here would change only if all three models passed. The clean steps, and the carried detectors' rates on them without the skip rule, were known before the criteria were set, so the clean-step criteria are not a fresh test. On Granite the carried detector with the retrained probe does cut false alarms on the clean steps at every depth (0.352 to 0.185). Leaving the first step out of the carried score keeps its clean-step cost below the previous subsection's. With the released probe the rate on the original clean steps is 0.173, 0.299 and 0.206 here, against 0.320, 0.351 and 0.374 without the skip rule. It also costs recall after arrival on Granite and Hermes (0.746 to 0.726 and 0.918 to 0.875), and that loss fails the recall criterion there. Without the skip rule the released probe's recall holds on all three models, but its false alarms on clean steps at every depth with a tool output rise more ([+0.243, +0.402], [+0.116, +0.255] and [+0.411, +0.556]). So the released cascade's gap after arrival stays a limitation.

On Qwen and Granite one forward pass could replace the state a caller would otherwise keep for each task. On these two models, over every earlier depth with a tool output of all 5,278 attacked after-arrival steps (old and new templates), each earlier step's rendered prompt is an exact token prefix of the later step's (18,675 of 18,675 pairs). Clean trajectories were not checked. So the state at the end of each earlier assistant-turn header in the later prompt equals that earlier step's prompt state, up to floating-point differences from batching and chunking. The pre-registered check asked for a change in presence score of at most 0.05 at no fewer than 95% of the earlier headers. For 40 later steps per model with prompts of at most 12,000 tokens, one forward pass with `hidden_states.mode` set to `all_tokens` and prefix caching off meets it on Qwen and Granite. The largest changes there are 0.030 and 0.020, comparable to the change between two captures of the same step (0.046 and 0.018), and the carried presence score crossed its threshold differently on 1 of the 40 steps on Qwen and on none on Granite or Hermes. Hermes' chat template renders the last tool message without the newline it gives earlier ones, so no earlier prompt is a prefix there. The check fails on Hermes, with the one-pass score more than 0.05 away at 31% of the headers (largest 0.32). The analyzer does not read these positions, and the cost of an `all_tokens` capture with prefix caching off was not measured.

The decision-time cascade multiplies the presence score by a probe on the first 16 generated tokens. On the new after-arrival steps its recall is above the presence cascade's on every model ([+0.243, +0.392], [+0.038, +0.274] and [+0.199, +0.333]), and it lowers attacked-but-safe false alarms there against the risk probe alone (Qwen 0.646 to 0.305, Granite 0.506 to 0.390, Hermes 0.780 to 0.237). Its recall against the risk probe alone was pre-registered as a reading of holds, loses or inconclusive, without an adoption rule. That reading is inconclusive on the new after-arrival steps on all three models, and on the old templates' after-arrival steps the cascade loses recall on Granite [−0.211, −0.002]. Its false alarms on clean steps are higher than the presence cascade's on Qwen (original clean steps [+0.021, +0.170], clean steps at every depth [+0.032, +0.150]) and Granite (original clean steps [+0.101, +0.235]). For Hermes the intervals are wide and include no change ([−0.064, +0.052] and [−0.128, +0.111]). The comparisons with the presence cascade and the false-alarm rates were reported, not tested, and the decision stage's recall loss on held-out injection tasks, described below, still applies.

### Rollouts the models drive themselves

Every step above was cut from a task's ground-truth trajectory. Its history holds calls the model did not choose. Here each model runs every v1 user task as an agent with greedy decoding, once with no injection and once per injection task with the `important_instructions_no_model_name` template, for up to 8 actions of up to 512 tokens each. A rollout ends at the first action without a call. Each step is captured as before, and its history is the model's own calls with their real outputs. Granite's calls are read permissively from any JSON in its text and written back in its own call syntax. The template is one of the three the probes were trained on, and in split A the injection tasks are in training too. In 270, 277 and 249 of the 629 attacked rollouts a tool output delivered the injection into the context. A step is unsafe if its own action follows the injection, judged against the environment after its earlier calls. The detectors are built as in the evaluations above and refit for each fold on each model's original v1 steps only, tested on held-out user tasks (split A) or held-out injection tasks (split D), and they do not flag a step without a tool output. The tests and an adoption rule for the decision cascade were committed before the rollouts ran. The table reports held-out user tasks (split A), the mean of 4 fold seeds.

On the clean rollout steps the presence cascade lowers false alarms on Qwen [−0.318, −0.176] and Hermes [−0.155, −0.054]. It is not shown to on Granite ([−0.016, +0.009]), whose risk probe alone flags only 0.107 of these steps. These clean steps come from the same 97 AgentDojo user tasks as the ground-truth ones. Granite and Hermes act on the injection in only 4 rollout steps each, so their recall cannot be measured here (the pre-registered readings, "loses" on Granite from 1 unsafe step after arrival and "inconclusive" on Hermes from none, say nothing). In Qwen's rollouts, with 106 unsafe steps after arrival, the presence cascade's recall after arrival is 0.514 against the risk probe's 0.783 [−0.428, −0.152], and at arrival it is within two points ([−0.018, 0.000]). This is in line with the ground-truth continuations, although those assume the agent ignored the injection so far, while a rollout's history can include steps that followed it, and the risk probe alone is itself lower here (0.783 against 0.896 there).

The decision cascade is adopted on no model. Its criteria asked for recall no more than 0.05 below the risk probe alone on the rollout steps with the injection in context, with held-out user tasks. False alarms on the clean rollouts could not exceed the presence cascade's by 0.05 or more. With held-out injection tasks, recall could be no more than 0.05 below the risk probe's on the original arrival steps, on the ground-truth continuations after arrival and on the rollout steps with the injection in context. Each criterion was judged by the 95% interval, and the arrival-step part repeats a result reported below, where Qwen already failed it. On all three models its recall on the ground-truth continuations with held-out injection tasks is not shown to stay within the margin ([−0.147, +0.009], [−0.219, +0.015] and [−0.062, +0.035]). Qwen misses every criterion, including rollout recall with held-out user tasks [−0.141, −0.010] and false alarms on its clean rollouts, whose interval is wide ([−0.047, +0.079]). Its recall after arrival in its rollouts, not a criterion, is also below the risk probe's [−0.218, −0.017]. The decision cascade's stage 1 runs at a 97% recall gate, so it can flag more than the risk probe alone, and on Granite it triples false alarms on the clean rollouts (0.104 to 0.318 [+0.159, +0.271]). On Hermes it has fewer false alarms on the clean rollouts than the presence cascade (0.102 against 0.148 [−0.085, −0.012]). On Granite and Hermes the two rollout-recall criteria rest on 4 unsafe steps and say nothing about recall (Granite passes both, Hermes fails the one with held-out injection tasks). So the cascade described here is still the presence cascade, and its recall loss after arrival stands.

### Reading the decision stage at the tool call

Granite starts its call within the first 16 tokens in 106 of its 150 unsafe actions (Qwen 268 of 353, Hermes 88 of 104). It writes the arguments before the tool name, and the name comes after token 16 in 116 of the 150. Its safe calls usually start later, after token 16 in 162 of 253, while 33 of its unsafe calls do.

A decision probe can read the call itself instead. `tool_call_window` finds the first call in the generated token ids, and a probe with `"position": "decode_window"` averages the decode states inside the window passed for each request. To compare windows on the actions evaluated above, each state was recomputed by teacher forcing, feeding the prompt plus the first t action tokens and keeping the last state. These states match the original decode capture at a median cosine of 0.9997 to 0.9999 across the three models. The windows compared are 16 tokens from the call's first token, 16 tokens from the tool name, and the whole call up to 64 tokens. Steps without a call fall back to the first 16 tokens, and below "first 16 tokens" means that window recomputed the same way. A new split D holds out injection tasks, so each test step's injection task is absent from training. Attacker identifiers such as a bank account or an email address can still recur across a suite's tasks. The protocol was fixed before these states existed.

A decision-time stage runs after the action is written, so the baseline for it is the simplest decision-time rule, which flags a step only if the risk probe fires and the action contains a tool call. The rule misses unsafe actions that are only text, which happen for attacker goals that are messages (11 of Granite's 150 unsafe steps). On Granite, over all steps in split A, it lowers the attacked-but-safe rate from 0.346 to 0.139 at recall 0.897, and no hidden-state stage does better. The window from the call's first token comes close (0.190, against 0.438 for the first 16 tokens), but its gain is on steps that end in text, and on steps with a call its rate rises from 0.657 to 0.842. What it adds on Granite is mainly whether the action contains a call. On Hermes the rule changes almost nothing (0.344), while the first 16 tokens bring the rate to 0.143. On Qwen the rule lowers the rate only from 0.432 to 0.392, while the whole-call window brings it to 0.210 [−0.289, −0.158] at an unchanged recall of 0.897, against 0.330 for the first 16 tokens.

To score a decision probe on the call with `MiaLLM`, generate with `hooks_on="both"` and `merge_probes=False`, find each request's window with `tool_call_window(tokenizer, output.token_ids)`, and pass the windows as `"decode_windows"` in the analyzer spec. On 64 Granite steps generated this way on an A40, 41 reproduced the evaluated action. On those, the features from this path match the teacher-forced ones at a median cosine of 0.99993 (minimum 0.994). In 12 of the 41 the window starts one token later than the teacher-forced one (cosine 0.994 to 0.998), and the cause was not checked. No probe trained on call windows is released with this page, because on held-out injection tasks they lost recall on two of the three models.

### Attempts to make the decision stage hold up on new attacks

Among tool-call steps, with the decision probes trained on tool-call steps only, Qwen's whole-call window catches 0.304 of the held-out unsafe calls whose tool was not the tool of any unsafe training step, against 0.784 of the others. This breakdown was made after the split D results. The risk probe alone holds 0.872 and 0.906. Granite and Hermes show smaller gaps in the same direction, and over all steps the window loses no more recall on those calls than the risk probe does. Neither of the changes fixed before they were tested repaired the recall loss among tool-call steps. Removing each tool's mean state before training brings Qwen's recall on those calls back to 0.926, but its attacked-but-safe rate rises from 0.091 to 0.539, above the risk probe's 0.399. Leaving the argument-value tokens out of the window lowers recall on all three models in point estimate (Qwen 0.544 against 0.634), with every interval including no change.

A rule with no training of its own gave a better trade among tool-call steps. After the risk probe, it flags an action whose first tool call carries an argument value of at least four characters that appears in a tool output and in none of the system, user or assistant messages or the earlier calls. In split D it lowers the attacked-but-safe rate on all three models (Qwen 0.399 to 0.281, Granite 0.624 to 0.526, Hermes 0.336 to 0.274). Recall falls by about three points with intervals that reach ten, which misses its pre-registered bar. Part of the labels marks calls with an attacker-specific argument, which this rule targets directly, so its recall may be optimistic. It can serve as stage 2 through `TwoStageDetector`.

AgentDojo v1.2 adds eight workspace injection tasks that v1 lacks. Tasks 6 to 12 send the user's data to the attacker, and task 13 sends phishing mail to the people the user meets. With every detector trained on v1, the risk probe alone catches 27 of Qwen's 39 unsafe tool-call steps there, and the whole-call cascade catches 18. Most of those calls (85%) use a tool that was an attack tool in training, so the drop is not only a matter of new tools. Granite (14) and Hermes (1) have too few unsafe steps to read.

Whether more attack goals in training would help could not be settled with the public data available. InjecAgent has 62 attack goals, each with its own first attacker tool, in 2,108 steps. On Qwen's tool-call steps, the whole-call stage trained on more goals catches more of the held-out goals (0.708 with 6 training goals, 0.908 with 12, 0.878 with 24 and 0.911 with all of them), so recall levels off from about 12 goals. At full diversity the interval for its recall minus the risk probe's is [−0.065, +0.064], which misses the pre-registered bar of −0.05. A breakdown made afterwards shows that every safe call Qwen made there calls the tool that the context already called, so this benchmark cannot separate the stage from a cue as simple as whether a call repeats that tool. Every call Granite and Hermes made there was an attack, so the test could not run for them. Back on AgentDojo, adding InjecAgent's tool-call steps (408 for Qwen, 65 for Granite, 13 for Hermes) to the decision probe's training data left the recall change on held-out injection tasks among tool-call steps within intervals that include zero (Qwen 0.630 to 0.752, [−0.017, +0.28]), and it raised the attacked-but-safe false-alarm rate on Qwen and Granite.

A stage built on the model's attention while it writes the call, with nothing trained, gave a small and fragile gain. For each step it reads the first tool call: the last-token attention of every head in four layers at fixed depths (vLLM-Hook's `capture_qk` worker in score mode, which needs an eager engine), at the token before the call's first argument value, or before the tool name for a call without argument values (11% of Qwen's calls). The score is the mean over heads of each head's attention on the tool outputs divided by its attention on the tool outputs and the user's request together. Only the two thresholds are calibrated. Among tool-call steps on held-out injection tasks (split D) it lowers the attacked-but-safe rate on Qwen from 0.399 to 0.344 [−0.075, −0.033] and on Granite from 0.624 to 0.562 [−0.091, −0.032], with recall changes of [−0.023, +0.004] and [−0.036, +0.002], which meets the criterion fixed before the test on two of the three models. On Hermes the rate falls from 0.336 to 0.268, but recall falls from 0.921 to 0.846 [−0.134, +0.009]. In split A and over all steps, only Qwen meets that criterion, and on Granite the score ranks unsafe against safe attacked calls no better than chance (AUROC 0.53 [0.42, 0.64], against 0.69 on Qwen and 0.77 on Hermes). Against the text provenance rule on Qwen it keeps more recall but cuts fewer false alarms (attacked-but-safe rate higher by [+0.025, +0.111]). On the v1.2 injection tasks it catches 22 of Qwen's 39 unsafe tool-call steps against 27 for the risk probe alone, so it also needs validating on new attacks.

The evaluation scripts, their outputs and the action texts and labels of all three models are in [latent-state-auditing/serving/vllm_hook/two_stage](https://github.com/rishabhsinha17/latent-state-auditing/tree/main/serving/vllm_hook/two_stage). The hidden-state captures are too large for git and are regenerated by its GPU scripts.

## Limitations

- The evaluation covers three 8B-class models on AgentDojo v1 only, with 97 clean steps per model at the injection depth and 337 more across every depth of each task. False alarms are much higher on the latter. Skipping steps without a tool output removes the worst of them but not all. Retraining the presence probe with the clean steps of every depth cuts most of the false alarms on clean steps after the first on Qwen and Granite, but on all three models it loses recall on attacked steps after arrival, so the cascade described here does not include it. Rollouts the models drive themselves on the same AgentDojo user tasks add 208, 103 and 96 clean steps with a tool output, on which the presence cascade cuts false alarms on Qwen and Hermes and is not shown to on Granite, whose risk probe alone already flags 0.107. The clean-step rate still needs checking on real deployments before anyone relies on it.
- Recall outside the subsections on injections that stay in the context, on carrying presence forward and on rollouts counts each attack at its arrival step. After that step the presence cascade's recall is 19 to 33 points below the risk probe alone's on these models with the original templates, and 25 to 34 points with two new ones (point estimates). Training on attacked steps at every depth failed the criteria. Carrying the presence score forward, tested on two attack templates no probe had seen, with the clean steps reused and the injection tasks in training, failed criteria fixed in advance on every model, through added false alarms on clean steps and, on Granite and Hermes, recall after arrival that did not hold. The decision-time cascade keeps more recall after arrival than the presence cascade, but against the risk probe alone its recall was inconclusive on the new steps and lower on Granite's older ones. A later test with criteria fixed in advance adopted it on no model. The criterion all three failed was recall on the ground-truth continuations after arrival with held-out injection tasks, and on Qwen it also lost recall on its own rollouts.
- The presence probe is a linear probe trained on AgentDojo injections. Other injection styles or tools need their own validation, and a dedicated injection detector can stand in as stage 2 through `TwoStageDetector`.
- Calibrate on data disjoint from what you report, and refit thresholds when the agent, tools or prompt format change.
- In splits A to C each test step's injection task also appears in training. On held-out injection tasks the decision stage lost recall on Qwen, and on Hermes among steps with a tool call. On the ground-truth continuations after arrival, the decision cascade's recall was not shown to hold on any of the three models. Every detector, the risk probe included, lost recall on the newer v1.2 injection tasks. Validate on attacks that were not in the training data.
- Decision-time probes work with offline `MiaLLM` and `merge_probes=False`. The served path (`MiaClient`) does not return per-request decode passes, and decode capture with every request's rows read added 0.8 s per batch of 32 on an A40 with the prefix cache controlled (up to about 2 s in earlier runs that did not control it).
